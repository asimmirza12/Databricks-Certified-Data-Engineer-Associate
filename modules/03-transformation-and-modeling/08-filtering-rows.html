Filtering rows

Filtering is choosing which rows to keep. It is the simplest transformation and the one you will write most often. The details are in how conditions combine, and in what happens to rows with missing values.

The idea

Ask a yes-or-no question of every row

A filter applies one test to each row. The test is a condition: a statement that is true or false for that row, such as "the price is above 3". Rows where it is true are kept. All others are left out.

You build bigger tests from small ones with three joining words:

  • AND: both parts must be true. Fewer rows pass.
  • OR: either part may be true. More rows pass.
  • NOT: the opposite of a test.

In SQL you write those words as they are, after WHERE. In PySpark you write & for AND, | for OR and ~ for NOT, inside .filter() or its twin .where().

Remember the rule from lesson 03-02. A test on a NULL value is neither true nor false. Since only true rows are kept, rows with a NULL in the tested column are dropped, whichever way round you ask.

Everyday analogy

A doorman with a checklist. "Over 18 AND on the guest list" lets in few. "Over 18 OR on the guest list" lets in many. And someone who cannot show any proof of age is not let in under "over 18", nor under "not over 18". The doorman simply cannot say. That person is the NULL.

How it works

True passes. False and unknown do not.

Filter early

Every later step has to carry the rows you keep. So put filters as early as you can, before joins and calculations. Spark's optimiser helps: it often moves a filter all the way down to the reading of the files, so that data which fails the test is never read at all. Giving it a clear, simple filter on a real column makes that possible.

Conditions you will write again and again


from pyspark.sql import functions as F

orders = spark.table("bakery.silver.orders")

# One condition. filter() and where() are the same method.
dear = orders.filter(F.col("price") > 3)

# AND is &, OR is |, NOT is ~. Each part MUST be in its own brackets.
north_dear   = orders.filter((F.col("shop") == "North") & (F.col("price") > 3))
north_or_big = orders.filter((F.col("shop") == "North") | (F.col("qty") >= 10))
not_north    = orders.filter(~(F.col("shop") == "North"))

# One of a list of values.
two_shops = orders.filter(F.col("shop").isin("North", "East"))

# A range, both ends included.
mid = orders.filter(F.col("price").between(2, 4))

# Text patterns.
b_items  = orders.filter(F.col("item").startswith("B"))
has_seed = orders.filter(F.col("item").contains("seed"))
like_b   = orders.filter(F.col("item").like("B%"))       # % stands for any characters

# Missing values need their own test.
no_price = orders.filter(F.col("price").isNull())

# A filter can also be written as a SQL string.
dear = orders.filter("price > 3 AND shop = 'North'")


SELECT * FROM bakery.silver.orders
WHERE shop IN ('North', 'East')
  AND price BETWEEN 2 AND 4
  AND item LIKE 'B%'
  AND order_date >= '2026-11-01';

-- Keep rows with no price as well as cheap ones.
SELECT * FROM bakery.silver.orders
WHERE price <= 3 OR price IS NULL;






  
What if I change this?

  
Small slips, wrong rows

  

    
ChangeEffect

    
AND to ORMore rows pass. A row now needs only one of the two tests to be true.

    
Leave the brackets off in PySpark: col("a") == 1 & col("b") > 2An error, or a wrong result. & is applied before == unless each part is bracketed.

    
Use Python's and / or between column conditionsAn error. Those words do not work on columns. Use & and |.

    
Filter on a column that has NULLsRows with NULL there are dropped, because the test is not true for them.

    
Negate the test with NOTThe NULL rows are still dropped. NOT of "unknown" is still "unknown".

    
BETWEEN 2 AND 4Both ends are included: 2 and 4 pass.

    
LIKE 'b%' on "Bagel"No match. Text comparison is case sensitive. Use ILIKE in SQL, or lower-case both sides.

    
Mix AND and OR without bracketsAND is applied first. a OR b AND c means a OR (b AND c). Add brackets to say what you mean.

    
Filter after a big join instead of beforeThe same result, but the join had to process rows that were about to be thrown away.

WHERE or HAVING?

  

    
WHERE
Filters individual rows, before any grouping. "Orders above 3 pounds."

    
HAVING
Filters groups, after totals are worked out. "Shops whose total is above 1,000." Lesson 03-11.

  






  
Real life

  
Filters that lose rows

  

    
ScenarioWhat happensFix

    
"Cancelled" plus "not cancelled" does not add up to all ordersOrders with a NULL status are in neither groupAdd OR status IS NULL to one of them, or fill the status

    
A filter for North shops returns nothingThe data says "north", and comparison is case sensitiveStandardise in silver, or compare on lower(shop)

    
A PySpark filter with two conditions throws a confusing errorThe brackets round each condition are missingBracket each part: (a) & (b)

    
A daily job reads three years of data and then keeps one dayThe date filter comes late, after expensive stepsFilter on the date first

    
A query meant to find "North, and either large or dear" returns South orders tooWritten as shop = 'North' AND qty > 10 OR price > 5, the OR part escapes the shop testBracket it: shop = 'North' AND (qty > 10 OR price > 5)

  






  
Don't confuse these

  
Filter traps

  

    
filter vs select
filter chooses rows. select chooses columns.

    
& | ~ vs and or not
On columns, only the symbols work. And every condition needs its own brackets.

    
NOT and NULL
A filter and its opposite do not always cover every row. Rows where the tested column is NULL fall through both.

    
WHERE vs HAVING
WHERE acts on rows before grouping. HAVING acts on groups after it.

  






  
Check yourself

  
Quiz

  

    

      1. A table has 10 rows. Three have a NULL price. Four have a price above 3. How many rows does WHERE NOT (price > 3) return?
      
      
      
      
Three rows have a price of 3 or less. The three NULL rows are not returned by the filter or by its opposite.

    

    

      2. Which PySpark filter is written correctly?
      
      
      
      
Use &, not and, and put each condition in its own brackets.

    

    

      3. Does price BETWEEN 2 AND 4 keep a row with price exactly 4?
      
      
      
      
BETWEEN includes both ends of the range.

    

    

      4. In the demo, what was the verdict on the Roll (price NULL) for "price > 3", and for NOT "price > 3"?
      
      
      
      
The test on NULL is unknown, and so is its opposite. Only true is kept.

    

    

      5. Why put a filter before a join where possible?
      
      
      
      
The result is the same, but less data moves through the expensive step.
